The Dickitry Clause

From the Mind of the Founder — a short introduction to a bigger NBL idea about words, context, and what someone has actually proven.

Page 1 of 3

01 / The thought

A correct sentence can still miss the truth.

Somebody can answer the exact words you used and still completely ignore what you meant. Sometimes that's an innocent misunderstanding. Sometimes it's a convenient way to avoid the real question. But we don't get to declare which one it is without evidence.

Being technically right about the words doesn't automatically mean you answered the point.

That's the territory of the Dickitry Clause. We look at the words, the circumstances, and the meaning the speaker had reason to understand. Then we check what the facts can actually support.

02 / The method

Don't just hear the answer. Examine it.

Before deciding who's right, put four things in their proper place.

1. The wordsWhat exactly was said? Don't rewrite the claim to make it easier to win.
2. The roomWhat did the situation and the conversation make those words mean?
3. The evidenceWhat has actually been demonstrated—and what hasn't?
4. The other possibilityCould this be an honest misunderstanding, a missing fact, or another reasonable interpretation?

The point isn't to catch everybody in a lie. It's to keep people—including ourselves—from treating a clever answer as a complete answer.
